# RESEARCH-INSIGHTS: what the Bloom Towers audit means

*First analytical position, written to be challenged. It's based on the corrected AUDIT.md (30 listings checked one by one, plus counts across all 208 1BR listings in Bloom Towers, JVC, on 3 Nov 2026). Section E ends on deciding which homes deserve a call, and that became my product thesis.*

Every claim below is tagged:
- **[Fact]:** directly observed and checkable
- **[Obs]:** a pattern across the evidence
- **[Interp]:** what I think it means
- **[Hyp]:** untested
- **[Rec]:** a recommendation

---

## A. What we believed, and what the evidence changed

| What we came in believing | What we found | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Fake listings are common, and "too cheap to be true" bait is the main pattern (the brief's framing) | **0 of 30 listings** met the bar for "misleading" on their own. No listing in the sample was priced below the building's normal range. The price outliers were all **above** the median (#1, #11, #24, #27). The furnished-sale percentages are approximate, because that median moves from 1,358 to 1,412 once duplicates are removed; #27 drops from +12% to +8% [Fact] | **Challenged** for this building and unit type |
| Duplicates exist | 5 of 15 sale listings are 2 flats (#21/#25/#26 and #19/#29), and one pair is AED 40,000 apart [Fact] | **Supported**, and more concrete than expected |
| The Verified badge signals a trustworthy listing | All 5 duplicate listings are Verified. That's consistent with what the badge actually checks (see D) [Fact] | **Refined.** The badge answers a narrower question than searchers probably assume |
| Similar-looking listings can be matched automatically | Most look-alike groups were different flats (#5/#6, #8/#9/#10, the four 673 sqft flats in Tower B) [Fact] | **Challenged.** Simple matching would flag honest agents |
| Stale listings are a problem | 23% of rent listings are older than three months, and #7 is about 23 months old, but the page shows "more than 6 months ago" [Fact]. Whether any of them is actually unavailable is **unknown** | **Not tested** |
| Agents push other units ("bait and switch") | That happens on the phone, not on the page. A page audit can't see it | **Not tested, and can't be from this method** |

**Surprises:**
- Holiday-home lets (#4, #7) sit among yearly leases. Their "annual rent" includes bills and leaves out booking fees, VAT and tourism tax [Fact].
- Paid placements are noticeably less often verified than standard listings (3 of 7 vs 9 of 163) [Fact, but on very small numbers].

**What we failed to find, and why it matters:** We found no confirmed misleading listing and no bait pricing. Either this building is cleaner than the brief assumes, or the trust problem lives somewhere a page can't show: availability and what happens when you call. Both possibilities point away from "detect fake listings" as the core of the product.

**What not to generalise:**
- This is one well-known building, one unit type (1BR), and the first 15 results in PF's default order.
- It says nothing about villas, off-plan, cheaper buildings, other emirates or other markets.
- The "no bait" result especially may not hold in a lower-priced building.

## B. What the searcher's actual problem is

**What the audit establishes:** some listings are duplicates, some prices are hard to compare, many claims carry little information, and availability can't be established from the page.
**What it doesn't establish:** how much time searchers lose, how often they contact unavailable flats, whether they struggle to spot duplicates, or whether they'd let Scout talk to agents for them. Everything below about the *searcher's need* is **[Hyp]** until tested.

My position on which *information problems* matter most, ranked by evidence [Interp]:

1. **The market looks bigger and more varied than it really is.** Duplicates shrink real choice: 15 sale listings were really 12 flats. On the rent side the evidence is weaker. Of 165 standard rent listings, 24 combinations of tower, size, price and furnishing repeat, covering 53 listings. Those are repeated **attributes**, not confirmed duplicates. Only 7 combinations show matching photos (at most 8 listings would disappear), and photo matching is weaker evidence there because one agency appears to reuse photos across flats (AUDIT, "Repeated rent listings"). Inflated claims shrink real information: half of all listings say "high floor" and almost none admits to a lower one. The searcher spends effort comparing things that are identical or meaningless. **Evidence: strong.**
2. **Prices aren't comparable as shown.** The same "yearly rent" can mean bills included or excluded, 1 to 12 cheques, extra fees or none (#4, #7, 166534211). Two prices for one flat (#19 vs #29). The number a searcher compares on isn't the number they'd actually pay. **Evidence: strong for the cases found. How common across the building is unknown.**
3. **Whether a flat is real *and available* is unknowable from the page.** Every listing's availability is unknown, and the age shown is capped at "more than 6 months". **Evidence: the gap is certain. How often it causes wasted effort is untested.**
4. **Outright deception on the page.** **Evidence: weak in this sample.** Two listings share one photo set but state different sizes (#22/#23). That's a strong integrity concern, not proven misrepresentation. One title also makes an unsupported superlative (#16). Real, but not the dominant problem here.

**What a searcher needs to know before pursuing a flat**, and where each answer can come from:

| Question | From the listing data? | Needs the agent? |
|---|---|---|
| Is this the same flat as another listing? | Partly (photos, size, price; only with care) | To be certain |
| What will I actually pay in year one? | Partly (cheques, furnishing, holiday-home terms in the text) | Fees, deposit, negotiable price |
| Is the price fair for this building? | Yes, roughly (price per sqft against the building), with known limits | No |
| Are the floor, view and photos true? | No (claims are noise, and photos can be reused) | Yes |
| Is it still available, and can I view it? | No | Yes |

**Where time and trust get lost:** comparing duplicates as if they were options; shortlisting on claims that mean nothing; contacting holiday-home lets thinking they're leases; and contacting agents about flats that may be gone, which is untested but likely.

## C. The stakeholders

**The searcher** [Obs]: carries the cost. More listings to read, prices that can't be compared, contacts that may go nowhere.

**Honest agents** [Interp]: also pay a cost they may not notice. When the same flat appears three times, their listing competes with copies of itself. When half the building claims "high floor", a real high floor is worth nothing in the listing. They'd gain from facts being trustworthy. A crude "suspicious" flag would hurt them most, since most look-alikes were legitimate (#5/#6, #8–#10).

**Agencies listing a flat that others also list** [Obs, plus Hyp on motive]: we saw several agencies advertising one flat at different prices. Under how Dubai brokerage works, several agents can legitimately hold permission to market the same flat. **We have no evidence of misconduct.** A price gap might be the owner giving different numbers, or room to negotiate. The incentive is clear, though [Hyp]: each extra copy is another chance at the lead, and the cost of the noise falls on the searcher.

**Property Finder** [Interp]: earns from agents' listings and placements, so more listings and paid placement mean revenue. Its long-term value depends on searchers trusting what they see. In this sample the two pull slightly apart: paid placements were less often verified (3/7 vs 9/163), and both holiday-home lets were paid placements. **That's an association on tiny numbers, not a claim that paid placement causes lower quality.** PF also already invests in trust: Verified, "great value" flags, permit display, price insights. So this isn't a gap PF ignores. It's a gap in what those tools cover.

## D. What "Verified" means here
**What PF says it verifies** [Fact, from PF's pages]:
- PF's quality-control team reviews documents for each listing: Form A, the title deed, the owner's ID and the advertising agreement.
- Verification lasts 45 days for rentals and 90 for sales, and can be renewed.
- Price isn't listed as checked on PF's partner page.

**What we can infer** [Interp]: at its last verification, the documents connecting this listing to an owner's authorisation were accepted. We don't know when that verification happened for any given listing.

**What the badge doesn't establish** [Obs, from this audit]:
- **Uniqueness:** several agencies' listings for one flat can each be verified (#21/#25/#26).
- **Consistent pricing:** two verified listings for one flat are 40,000 apart (#19/#29).
- **That the photos show this flat:** #22/#23 are verified and share photos.
- **Live availability.**

**The fair argument:** this doesn't mean verification is misleading or ineffective. Verification and transaction readiness answer **different questions**. A searcher can reasonably read the badge as "this listing passed PF's document check". They can't read it as "this is the only listing, the right price, accurately photographed and available today". The opportunity is in the second set of questions, which the badge was never designed to answer.

## E. What this means for the product

| Direction | Strength of evidence | Who already covers it | My read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Listing integrity:** duplicates, reused photos, contradictory fields | **Strong** for duplicates (photo-confirmed). Weak frequency data for the rest | Partly: Verified covers authorisation, not uniqueness | **A real opportunity, but mostly a platform fix.** Grouping duplicates is better done in search for everyone than in one person's assistant. The assistant's part is explaining a price conflict and asking which price is real |
| **2. Commercial transparency:** real yearly cost, cheques, fees, terms | **Strong** for the cases found (#4, #7, #19/#29). Unknown frequency | Partly: price insights show building averages, but not like-for-like terms | **The strongest opportunity for an assistant.** It needs a mix of page data and an agent's answers, which is exactly what the brief describes |
| **3. Transaction readiness:** availability, viewings, agent-confirmed facts | **Gap certain, size untested.** We don't know how often listings are unavailable | Not covered, except by calling the agent yourself | **Potentially the highest value, but the least proven.** Must not be presented as an audit finding |

**Testing the "worth pursuing" idea** [Hyp, not an audit finding]: the evidence fits a searcher whose real need is less *finding* flats and more **deciding which handful deserve a call**. That's supported by duplicates, comparability and claim noise. It isn't yet supported by any measure of wasted calls. If availability turns out to be rarely a problem, this idea is much weaker.

## Challenging this position

**Alternative readings of the same evidence:**
1. **Duplicates are just how the market works.** Several agents competing for one flat may help searchers find the best agent or price. The issue is presentation, which PF could fix in search, not something an assistant needs to do.
2. **The "pricing anomalies" are legitimate premiums.** Price per sqft ignores floor, view, layout and condition, which can justify +10–25%. We may be calling quality an anomaly.
3. **We picked a clean building.** Bloom Towers is established and highly verified (158 of 170 rent listings verified). The brief's trust problems might be concentrated in cheaper buildings, villas or off-plan. Our "no bait" result may be a selection effect.
4. **The real trust problem is behaviour, not listings.** Bait and switch, unanswered messages and pushy agents happen in the conversation. A listing audit structurally can't see them, so our evidence understates the problem the brief describes.

**The strongest argument against the emerging direction:** an assistant that talks to agents for the searcher adds a step to a market where speed matters. Good flats go fast, and agents prioritise direct, high-intent leads. Agents are PF's paying customers and may see a gatekeeper as a threat. Meanwhile, the clearest problem we proved (duplicates) is better fixed once at platform level. And most listings we checked were fine (12 trusted outright; most "checks" are questions, not warnings), so a filter would remove little.

**What a searcher can already do without Scout:**
- compare prices within a building
- spot obvious duplicates by eye
- message the agent to ask about availability and terms
- check the permit
- use PF's price insights

All of it is possible. It's slow and repetitive.

**Already covered by PF:** permit display, Verified, "great value" and price-drop flags, building price insights, the Report button, and Scout's search itself.

**Possible friction:** flags that make honest agents look suspicious; asking the searcher to approve every agent message; adding a step before a viewing; too many caveats on every listing.

**Noticed, but not a product opportunity:** the cheques field missing while the title states it (#3); one agent field with no person's name (#2); the furnishing mismatch on #30; the unsupported superlative (#16). These are real but small, and better handled by listing-quality checks than by a searcher's assistant.

**What would change my mind:**
- Contacting agents about 10–20 Bloom listings shows most are available, with clear answers. Then transaction readiness weakens.
- A cheaper building shows clear bait pricing. Then the brief's framing returns.
- Searcher interviews show they already ignore duplicates and claims. Then integrity matters less than we think.

## Prioritised insights

| # | Insight | Evidence | Confidence and limits | Product question it raises |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Searchers see more choice than exists.** Duplicates and identical-looking listings inflate the market | #21/#25/#26 and #19/#29 confirmed by photos (5 sale listings, 2 flats). On the rent side, 7 repeated combinations show matching photos (15 listings), with the photo-reuse caveat | **High** for the sale sample. **Low–medium** for rent | Should the assistant show *flats* rather than *listings*, and how does it treat a price conflict without blaming an agent? |
| 2 | **Prices aren't comparable as shown.** Terms change what a listed price means | #4 and #7 (holiday homes with bills included and extra fees), 166534211 (bills, 12 cheques), #19/#29 (two prices for one flat), cheques ranging from 1 to 12 | **Medium-high** for the cases. **Unknown** frequency | Can the assistant show "what you'd pay in year one" like-for-like, and which inputs need the agent? |
| 3 | **Listing claims are close to noise; structured facts and agent answers carry the signal** | 104 of 208 say "high floor"; 2 mention a lower floor, both in keyword lists; 20 claim a Marina view; one photo set on two listings of different sizes (#22/#23) | **High** that the claims are everywhere; says nothing about whether they're true | Should the assistant ignore claims, ask the agent instead, or show "agent-stated" vs "confirmed"? |
| 4 | **Verified answers "authorised", not "unique, correctly priced, available"** | PF's own documentation, plus Verified duplicates and Verified photo reuse | **High** on what the badge covers. Not a criticism of PF's process | What does a searcher need *on top of* Verified, and should the assistant explain the difference? |
| 5 | **The biggest untested gap is availability and agent behaviour, which happen off the page** | Availability unknown for all 30. #7's age hidden behind "more than 6 months". The brief describes the problem, but our method can't observe it | **Low.** It's a hypothesis | How often do enquiries about Bloom listings fail, and how much does the searcher lose when they do? |

**What we still need before committing to a solution:**
1. A small availability test: ask agents about about 10 Bloom listings and record response, time and answer. Even 5 would be a signal.
2. A 15-minute check of one cheaper JVC building to test whether "no bait" holds.
3. Direct evidence of what searchers actually do with duplicates and claims. Even 2–3 people would help.
